## Power Factor Correction — Why It Matters

Power Factor (PF) is the ratio of real power (kW) consumed to apparent power (kVA) drawn from the grid. Most inverter-based systems and inductive household loads (motors, AC compressors, LED drivers without correction) create a lagging power factor, typically 0.7–0.85 without correction[11].

**Why it is a product requirement:**

- Indian DISCOMs levy **PF penalty surcharges** on commercial and increasingly on high-consumption residential connections where PF falls below 0.90[1][11].
- A grid-tied bidirectional inverter that injects power with poor PF can attract reactive energy charges and may fail DISCOM interconnection approval[13].
- CEA (Grid Interactive Distributed Solar Energy Systems) regulations require grid-tied systems to maintain PF ≥ 0.95 under normal operating conditions[13][11].
- Active PFC in the inverter stage or as a dedicated module improves grid stability and makes the system eligible for net-metering approval in more states[13].

**Design requirement:** The bidirectional inverter shall include an active PFC stage targeting PF ≥ 0.95 at 50–100% load. The PFC module cost is budgeted at ₹6,000–₹10,000 and should be integrated into the inverter PCB at scale rather than added as a standalone component to reduce BOM[11][10].

## System Components Attached to Rooftop Solar

A complete rooftop solar installation in India comprises five major physical subsystems. Understanding each component's role, cost, and failure mode is essential for designing a retrofit storage product that integrates cleanly.

### Solar Panels

Mono PERC panels dominate the Indian residential market in 2026[11][12]. A 3 kW system requires 6–10 panels depending on wattage (300–500 Wp each), needing roughly 300–400 sq. ft. of shade-free roof area[15][16]. India-made panels from manufacturers such as Waaree, Adani Solar, and Vikram Solar cost ₹15,000–₹20,000/kW before mounting hardware[11][12]. Panel degradation is typically warranted at ≤0.7% per year with a 25-year output guarantee, though substandard panels from unverified suppliers degrade significantly faster[17][18].

### Charge Controllers

In grid-tied residential solar systems (the dominant setup under PM Surya Ghar), a dedicated charge controller is **not required** — the MPPT (Maximum Power Point Tracking) function is built into the solar inverter[19][20]. Standalone MPPT or PWM charge controllers are only relevant in off-grid or hybrid battery setups where the battery charges via a DC-coupled path, separate from the inverter[19][21]. In an AC-coupled retrofit storage system (like the India competitor product proposed here), the charge controller role is performed by the battery inverter's internal MPPT or AC-coupling logic, not a separate external unit[3][4]. For off-grid or hybrid setups with external MPPT controllers, India-made units cost ₹1,500–₹6,000 (PWM, 20–40A) to ₹8,000–₹25,000 (MPPT, 40–75A, LFP-compatible)[22][19][21].

### Inverter

The inverter converts DC power from the panels into 230V AC for household use and, in hybrid configurations, manages battery charge/discharge[17][23]. In a standard grid-tied setup, a **string inverter** (₹18,000–₹35,000 for 3–5 kW) is used. Retrofit storage systems require either replacing this with a **hybrid inverter** (₹35,000–₹70,000) or adding an **AC-coupled battery inverter** alongside the existing string inverter — the latter is the plug-in retrofit approach Anker uses[3][7]. The inverter is widely reported as the single most failure-prone component in Indian installations[17][23]. Common causes include thermal derating at ambient temperatures above 45°C (common in North and Central India in summer), poor ventilation in installation, substandard IGBT modules in low-cost units, and grid voltage fluctuations causing overvoltage trips[23][24].

### Fuse / Breaker Box

Every grid-tied solar installation in India requires:
- A **DC fuse/string combiner box** on the panel-to-inverter DC side, with fuses or MCCBs rated for the PV string voltage (typically 500–1000V DC)[25][26]
- An **AC circuit breaker** (MCB/MCCB, typically 16–32A) between the inverter and the household distribution board[25][27]
- A **bidirectional net metering unit** installed by the DISCOM between the household board and the grid connection[28][29]

Installation errors in breaker boxes are a documented source of failures — reverse wiring of AC circuit breakers is a known issue in India, where installers use MCBs not explicitly rated for reverse entry[25][27][26]. This can cause overheating, arcing, or premature tripping under high solar generation conditions. The proposed product's installation standard should mandate correct breaker orientation and breaker ratings verified against the bidirectional inverter's rated AC output current[25][26].

## Are These Custom Installations?

**Yes — almost all residential rooftop solar installations in India are custom, site-specific jobs**, not standardised product deployments[30][31][18]. Each installation varies on:

- **Roof type and area**: RCC, metal sheet, or asbestos (the latter cannot support panel weight safely)[15]
- **Panel string configuration**: Depends on roof orientation, shading profile, and inverter MPPT input ranges
- **Cable runs**: Determined by distance from roof to inverter/DB location — longer runs require larger cable gauges to limit resistive losses
- **DISCOM interconnection requirements**: Each state's electricity board has different technical and documentation standards for net metering approval[28][29]
- **Load profile**: System sizing depends on household consumption patterns, which vary significantly

This customisation creates several problems: inconsistent quality, high variability in installer skill, and no standardised commissioning checklist[30][31][18]. The competitor product should aim to **productise the install** — a fixed mounting kit, pre-wired AC coupling interface, and a digital installation wizard that guides even semi-skilled installers through DISCOM-standard commissioning steps.

## Tripping Statistics and Grid Interaction Issues

Inverter tripping is one of the most commonly reported post-installation problems across Indian rooftop solar forums and industry reports[30][23][24]. Causes and frequencies:

| Cause | Frequency | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Grid overvoltage (>253V) | Very common in urban feeders during low-load periods | Inverter disconnects; generation lost until grid normalises[23][32] |
| Grid undervoltage (<184V) | Common in semi-urban/rural feeders, summer afternoons | Inverter trips; backup unavailable without storage[23][33] |
| Grid frequency deviation (<49.0 or >50.5 Hz) | Moderate; worsens during peak generation hours | Inverter shuts down per CEA grid code compliance[32][33] |
| Thermal shutdown (inverter overtemperature) | Common May–June in North/Central India at 45°C+ ambient | Derating or full shutdown; significant generation loss[23] |
| DC arc fault / string mismatch | Periodic; mainly in poorly installed systems | Overcurrent trips, potential fire hazard[23] |
| Reverse-wired breaker failure | Post-installation; latent defect | Arcing, MCB failure, system shutdown[25][26] |

Industry observers report that many grid-tied inverters in India trip **10–30 times per month** during summer peak-generation months due to voltage fluctuations[23][24][33]. This means solar owners with grid-tied-only systems lose generation precisely when the panels are working hardest. A hybrid storage system with a **wide voltage ride-through range** (e.g., 176–264V AC) and **frequency ride-through** (47–52 Hz) would directly address this pain point and retain generation that grid-tied-only systems forfeit[3][23][32].

## System Sizing: Over- and Under-Dimensioning

Incorrect system sizing is cited as one of the top post-installation complaints in India[17][34][18]. Both over-dimensioning and under-dimensioning impose real financial and operational costs.

### Under-Dimensioning (More Common)

Installers sometimes size systems conservatively to reduce upfront cost or because shading and roof area are not properly assessed[17][34]. A household consuming 400 units/month with a 2 kW system (generating ~240 units/month) will still pay ₹15,000–₹20,000 annually in evening grid bills and see a much longer payback period[1][17]. Under-dimensioning also means the battery — if added later — is undercharged and cannot cover evening loads effectively.

### Over-Dimensioning (Also Common)

Over-sized systems produce surplus generation that cannot be exported if net metering is pending (a 50–60 day average delay[28]) or if DISCOM export limits are applied (many states cap export at 90% of sanctioned load). This results in **curtailed generation, wasted investment, and no bill credit** for the over-produced units[28][29]. A 5 kW system on a household consuming 300 units/month will waste roughly 30–40% of its output if storage is not present[1][34].

### Sizing Recommendation for Competitor Product

The product should include a **digital load assessment and sizing tool** as part of onboarding. This tool should:
- Ingest 3–6 months of DISCOM electricity bills
- Map roof area and orientation via a mobile app (photo + GPS)
- Recommend panel capacity, battery size, and inverter rating based on actual consumption and shading profile
- Flag if the proposed system would exceed DISCOM export limits, triggering a storage-first recommendation[17][34][28]

## Pain Points Today

Based on cross-industry reporting, the primary post-installation pain points for Indian rooftop solar owners are[30][17][31][18][23][28]:

- **System underperformance vs. promise**: The most common complaint — systems generate less than projected due to incorrect sizing, shading not accounted for, or low-quality panels. Homeowners have no easy way to verify this without monitoring[17][18].
- **Inverter failures and tripping**: Frequent voltage/frequency-related trips reduce effective generation, especially in summer. Low-grade inverters fail within 2–3 years[30][17][23].
- **Net metering delays**: Average DISCOM meter replacement takes 50–60 days; subsidy disbursement can take 6–24 months[28]. During this period, surplus generation is lost with no compensation.
- **After-sales abandonment**: Installers become unresponsive within 3–12 months of installation. No standardised maintenance schedule is provided[30][31][18].
- **Lack of monitoring**: Most homeowners cannot tell how much their system generates vs. what is consumed vs. exported. No app, no alerts, no fault notifications[17][31].
- **No backup during outages**: Grid-tied solar shuts down during outages (anti-islanding protection). Homeowners who bought solar expecting backup are surprised when panels produce nothing during a power cut[3][17].
- **Quality variability**: No standardised installer certification; system quality varies enormously by vendor[31][18].

## Electricity Bill Pain Points

Even after solar installation, middle-class Indian households continue to face significant electricity bill friction[1][28][29]:

- **Evening and night grid dependency**: Without storage, 40–50% of consumption (evening loads — fans, lighting, TV, cooking) is still drawn from the grid at full tariff rates[1][3]. This is the largest remaining bill line item after solar.
- **Slab-rate escalation**: Indian residential tariffs use consumption slabs. A 400-unit/month household sits in higher tariff slabs (₹8–₹10/unit) for evening grid units, exactly where storage has the highest savings impact[1].
- **Delayed or denied net metering credits**: If the DISCOM net metering connection is delayed or if the export limit is breached, surplus daytime solar generation earns nothing — effectively a zero-revenue stranded asset during that period[28][29].
- **Fixed / demand charges**: Many DISCOMs now levy fixed monthly charges (₹100–₹500/month) irrespective of consumption, which solar cannot offset. A battery that enables demand shifting can partially address this by reducing peak demand[1].
- **Poor subsidy disbursement**: PM Surya Ghar subsidies are disbursed post-installation and post-inspection. Homeowners must fund the full cost upfront and wait months for the credit, creating cash-flow pressure[28][2].
